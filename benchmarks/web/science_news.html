Who checks the checkers?

A test of peer review at hundreds of open access journals

By A. Reporter · 4 Oct 2013 · Vol 342, Issue 6154 · pp. 60-65 · DOI: 10.1126/science.0000000

A paper designed to fail

Every year, thousands of new journals appear that promise fast publication in return for a fee paid by the author. Many of them claim to run careful peer review, but few people have checked whether that is true. Every year, thousands of new journals appear that promise fast publication in return for a fee paid by the author. Many of them claim to run careful peer review, but few people have checked whether that is true.

To find out, we wrote a scientific paper with obvious errors in its methods and results. Any reviewer with a basic training in chemistry should have spotted the problems within minutes. To find out, we wrote a scientific paper with obvious errors in its methods and results. Any reviewer with a basic training in chemistry should have spotted the problems within minutes.

We then sent versions of the paper to more than three hundred open access journals over a period of ten months. Each version used a different invented author and a different invented institution. We then sent versions of the paper to more than three hundred open access journals over a period of ten months. Each version used a different invented author and a different invented institution.

More than half of the journals accepted the paper. In many cases the acceptance came without any sign that a reviewer had read the text at all, and some editors asked only for changes to the layout. More than half of the journals accepted the paper. In many cases the acceptance came without any sign that a reviewer had read the text at all, and some editors asked only for changes to the layout.

The results suggest that the label 'peer reviewed' is not always a guarantee of quality. Readers, librarians and funders need better ways to judge which journals do the work they promise.
